# Turning a local shop, pub or service into a co-op

A step-by-step guide for community groups in Sussex.

**From:** Morality Co-operative Ltd. **Version:** draft, 20 October 2026.

This guide summarises the route. It is not legal or financial advice. The authoritative guidance is published by Co-operatives UK, the Community Shares Unit, Plunkett UK and the FCA; links are given at each step and listed at the end. Rules and fees change, so check the current versions before you act.

---

## Before you start: is a co-op the right answer?

A co-op works when enough local people care about the place to join it, put money into it and, often, volunteer. Ask yourselves:

- Can we name at least 10–20 people prepared to do real work over the next year?
- Is the business viable with the trade it is likely to get, or only with permanent subsidy?
- Is the building or business actually available, and at what price?
- Who else is already trying to save it?

If the answers are shaky, spend a month on them before anything else. Plunkett UK supports community shops and pubs specifically and offers early advice. Co-operatives UK runs a start-up advice service and can tell you what support is currently funded.

## Step 1. Form a steering group (weeks 1–4)

- Hold an open public meeting. Record who comes and who offers to help.
- Form a steering group of five to nine people with a mix of skills: finance, law or property, retail or hospitality, publicity, and someone who knows the neighbourhood well.
- Agree a short written purpose ("to keep the Red Lion open as a pub owned by the village") and how the group will make decisions until the co-op exists.
- Open a simple shared record of decisions and money spent. You will need it later.

**If the asset is under threat:** ask your district or unitary council to list the building as an **Asset of Community Value** (Localism Act 2011). If it is listed and the owner tries to sell, a community group can trigger a six-month moratorium to raise money and bid. The English Devolution and Community Empowerment Bill proposes a stronger community right to buy; check whether it is in force when you apply.

## Step 2. Choose the legal form (weeks 2–6)

Most community-owned shops, pubs and services in England register as a society under the **Co-operative and Community Benefit Societies Act 2014**, registered by the FCA. There are two types.

| | Co-operative society | Community benefit society (BenCom) |
|---|---|---|
| Who it serves | Its members (staff, customers or both) | The wider community |
| Typical use | Worker co-ops, consumer co-ops, multi-stakeholder co-ops | Community pubs, shops, energy, buildings |
| Asset lock | Optional | Can adopt a statutory asset lock, so assets cannot be sold off for members' private gain |
| Community shares | Yes | Yes |
| Charitable status | No | Possible (exempt charity) if purposes are charitable |

**Rule of thumb:** if the point is to save something for the whole community, choose a **community benefit society with an asset lock**. If the point is for the people who work in or shop at the business to own and run it, choose a **co-operative society**. Many grant funders and the Community Shares Standard Mark expect an asset lock on community assets.

Other forms (community interest company, company limited by guarantee) are possible, but they cannot issue withdrawable community shares, which is the main way community pubs and shops raise money.

Both society types need **at least three members** to register. One member, one vote is the norm regardless of how many shares a member holds.

## Step 3. Adopt model rules (weeks 4–8)

The rules are the society's constitution. Do not write them from scratch.

- **Co-operatives UK** and **Plunkett UK** publish model rules for co-operative societies and community benefit societies, including versions designed for community shares.
- Registering on a sponsoring body's model rules, with only the permitted amendments, is quicker and costs less in FCA fees than registering bespoke rules. The sponsoring body charges its own fee.
- Decide in advance: membership eligibility (for example, anyone over 16 living in the parish), minimum shareholding, board size and term lengths, how members are told about meetings, and whether staff can be members.

## Step 4. Register with the FCA (weeks 6–12)

- Submit the application through the FCA's online system for registered societies, usually via the sponsoring body if you use its model rules.
- You will need: the society's name, registered office, the rules, three founder members and a secretary.
- Once registered, the society appears on the **FCA Mutuals Public Register** and has limited liability.
- Check the FCA's current fees and timescales on its registered-societies pages before budgeting.

## Step 5. Business plan and money (months 2–6)

Write a business plan that a sceptical outsider would believe. It should cover purchase or lease cost, refurbishment, opening stock, 12–36 months of trading forecasts, staffing, and what happens if trade is 20% below forecast.

Typical funding mix for a community pub or shop:

1. **Community shares** from members (see Step 6)
2. **Grants**, for example from the strategic authority's co-operative development money once it is allocated, or from national and local funds. Check which are open; many schemes have closed or changed since 2024.
3. **Loans** from social lenders or, sometimes, the vendor
4. **Pre-trading help** such as free advice days from Co-operatives UK or Plunkett UK

## Step 6. Run a community share offer (months 4–8)

Community shares are withdrawable, non-transferable shares in a registered society.

- Shares do not rise in value. Members may be paid modest interest once the business can afford it, and may be able to withdraw their money later on terms set by the board.
- The law caps an individual's holding of withdrawable shares (currently £100,000 under the 2014 Act). Most offers set a minimum of £50–£250 and a much lower maximum.
- Community share offers are largely exempt from FCA financial-promotion rules. That means members have **no** access to the Financial Ombudsman Service or the Financial Services Compensation Scheme. Your offer document must say so plainly.
- Follow the **Community Shares Handbook**. It separates what you *must* do by law from what you *should* do as good practice.
- Consider applying for the **Community Shares Standard Mark**, awarded after independent assessment by a Licensed Community Shares Practitioner. Investors and match funders treat it as a sign the offer is sound. Smaller offers (under about £50,000) often do without it.
- Check whether match funding or practitioner support is available, for example through Co-operatives UK.

## Step 7. Buy, open and trade (months 6–12)

- Complete the purchase or lease. Take legal advice on title, licences (premises licence and designated premises supervisor for a pub), planning and business rates relief.
- Arrange insurance: employer's liability, public liability, buildings, contents and directors' and officers' cover.
- Decide early whether volunteers or paid staff run the business day to day, and write it down. Most community pubs are tenanted or managed; most community shops mix one or two paid staff with volunteers.

## Step 8. First-year governance

The first year is when most avoidable problems start. Keep it simple and written down.

**Legal duties**
- Keep a **register of members and officers** and a register of share holdings.
- Hold an **annual general meeting** as your rules require; elect or re-elect board members.
- Submit an **annual return with accounts to the FCA** within seven months of your financial year end.
- Check whether your rules and turnover require an audit or an accountant's report.

**Good practice**
- Board meets monthly in year one; minutes published to members within two weeks.
- A conflicts-of-interest register, updated at every meeting.
- Clear split between the board (strategy, money, oversight) and whoever runs the business day to day.
- A member communications plan: at least a quarterly update and one social event.
- A simple dashboard for members: sales, margin, cash in bank, volunteer hours, number of members.
- Plan for board succession from the start. Founders burn out.
